# Evidence behind Sam's writing profile

This report covers the original computer-file review. The September 18, 2026 [Gmail evidence report](sam-writing-gmail-evidence.md) adds stronger evidence for everyday correspondence and updates the conclusions in the [current profile](../SKILL.md). Read this report for longer explanations and reflections; use the Gmail report for email style.

The strongest recurring pattern is explanatory reasoning with a personal point of view. Sam tends to make the workings of a situation explicit, compare an idea against a real example, and identify the condition that limits it. His voice has more connective language, more qualification, and more room for explanation than a generic guide to concise professional writing would capture.

The accompanying [profile](../SKILL.md) turns these findings into drafting instructions. The [corpus audit](sam-writing-corpus-audit.md) identifies every source by file number and records how it was treated.

## Scope and method

All 114 files listed for review were opened locally. Their complete extracted text was read, including duplicate copies, tables, references, prompts, and document headers where present. The files comprise 76 Word documents, 8 PDFs, and 30 Markdown files. Text extraction produced roughly 64,500 whitespace-separated tokens before removing duplicates, copied material, formatting artifacts, or non-prose content. That figure is a reading-volume estimate, not a count of words attributable to Sam.

The review also processed 245 embedded image assets through local OCR and read the recognized text. All 54 assets with no recognized text were inspected visually. Most were highlight marks, line drawings, or photographs; a few were formulas or a small numeric table. Native chart text was inspected separately. OCR is imperfect on tiny labels, equations, and screenshots, so this review does not claim a verified character-perfect transcription of every graphic. The profile's quotations come from document text, not uncertain OCR.

Only files on the handoff's review lists were read. The excluded project locations and sensitive-record categories were not searched. Source files were not edited or uploaded. External articles and videos linked from the samples were not part of the reading corpus; the link-only senior-design document was read as a link-only document.

Authorship was assessed at the passage level. A name in document metadata does not establish who wrote every sentence. Instructor questions, cited quotations, copied definitions, group reports, templates, and another writer's narration about Sam were not treated as equivalent to individual prose. The quoted historical claims below are evidence of expression, not current factual advice or verification of their subject matter.

## What the individual writing shows

### 1. Sam thinks aloud through causes and consequences

In the Production Systems assignment, he follows an operational choice to its failure mode:

> They were running so close to the edge that they were operating at peak efficiency, however because of that they lost their resilience.

File 11, final body paragraph. File 55 is the same text.

In the Beer Game reflection, he describes how his own local decision affected the rest of the system:

> Reflecting on my own behavior, I can see how my ever-increasing order quantity caused some panic in the distributor's minds and caused them to increase order quantity as well.

File 64, paragraph beginning "Even though mistakes were made". Apostrophe typography normalized here.

The distinctive behavior is the explanation between the concept and the outcome. It appears in supply-chain analysis, marketing critiques, technical homework, and reflection. A faithful draft should retain those intermediate steps.

Confidence is high. These are repeated behaviors across individual documents, not conclusions based only on Sam describing himself as a systems thinker.

### 2. He translates concepts into situations the reader can picture

The later Design of Everyday Things review connects root-cause analysis to a family example:

> Norman's emphasis on design thinking brings to mind an example of a house with a leaking roof. This once happened to a relative of mine.

File 3, second paragraph. Apostrophe typography normalized.

He then explains that repainting a discolored ceiling addressed the visible symptom while the roof kept causing other problems. The example makes the concept understandable before he returns to its importance.

The same behavior appears in file 5's comparison between crowdfunding and donating to people or charities, file 9's use of a soup-bowl experiment to reason about leftovers, and file 57's job-search scenario to examine photo removal. Some illustrations are remembered experiences and some are hypothetical. The new writer must preserve that distinction.

Confidence is high for concrete examples. The corpus does not support making every example autobiographical.

### 3. His judgments are personal without being purely subjective

Sam repeatedly uses "I believe," "I think," "personally," and "in my mind" before explaining why he holds a view. In the marketing assignment about human and robotic labor:

> Although I'm not completely sure how an algorithm designs a poster, I found it interesting to see that there was still a significant difference in the human-designed poster, even though both were printed on machinery.

File 8, response to question 2. Apostrophe typography normalized.

This combines curiosity, an explicit knowledge limit, and attention to a controlled comparison. It is not a posture of effortless expertise. His follow-up asks for qualitative questions to understand the participants' reasons.

Confidence is high. A draft that removes all first-person qualifiers would be smoother but less representative.

### 4. He looks for the condition where a claim stops working

In the attribute-tradeoff marketing critique, Sam asks whether the effect would survive a change in the size of the tradeoff:

> In this study, good and bad was decided comparatively to the other options provided, however I wonder if there exists a threshold to where the good condition is too low or the bad condition is too high, where consumers begin to break these tendencies.

File 7, response to question 3.

In a system-dynamics model comparison, he names omitted variables:

> Both models are still limited, however, as they don't account for other significant variables such as students dropping out of school or switching out of engineering majors.

File 65, "Model Comparison". Apostrophe typography normalized.

This is an especially useful instruction for imitation: qualify the conclusion by naming what changes it. Avoid adding a vague disclaimer after an otherwise unexamined claim.

Confidence is high across critique, modeling, and ethical argument. The assignment prompts sometimes explicitly request limitations, so the frequency is partly genre-driven. The specificity of his answers is still meaningful.

### 5. He can recommend against an attractive option

The Italy executive summary first explains why the market appears promising, then makes a clear recommendation:

> Given the already huge market, Creamy would be a great fit in Italy; however, I don't believe that Creamy should enter.

File 45, third paragraph; the PDF in file 4 contains the same prose. Apostrophe typography normalized.

He explains the competition and difficulty of differentiation, then describes how entry could work if it were pursued anyway. This is a willingness to weigh an alternative and still choose. It should not be flattened into a neutral list of pros and cons.

Confidence is high within analytical recommendations. It does not establish that every message should begin with the conclusion; this particular document builds context first.

### 6. Reflection often shows the path from resistance to understanding

The final English reflection opens:

> Admittedly, I'm not an English guy.

Later:

> Well, I thought it was unfortunate.

And later still:

> Simply put, writing is important.

File 20, opening two paragraphs. Apostrophe typography normalized.

The short sentences punctuate longer explanations. The change of view follows the course experience and Sam's realization that communication is necessary for engineering work. Similar movement occurs in file 25, where discomfort with a comic-book textbook becomes appreciation of its format.

Confidence is high for reflective writing. It would be a mistake to turn every work update into an account of personal growth.

### 7. His humor is candid and situational

The Pink Time reflection ends:

> As far as grading goes, I would be an idiot to not give myself 100%. I feel like I was able to learn a lot, and it's a free opportunity to get some points.

File 46, final paragraph. Apostrophe typography normalized.

File 20 contains a brief aside about walking into the wrong building. These moments are direct and self-aware. The humor follows naturally from what happened or what is being asked.

Confidence is moderate because relatively few individual samples show humor. The fraternity interview-question collection contains a much more provocative register, but its original authorship is unclear and it should not define Sam's default voice.

### 8. He favors consistent words over polished variation

In file 26, the research reflection repeatedly uses "research," "sources," and "I believe." In file 64, he repeatedly uses "supply chain," "order," "demand," and "communication." The reader does not have to infer whether a new synonym refers to the same thing.

This sometimes becomes redundant, especially in the early academic work. The useful rule is to preserve stable terms and explanatory restatement while trimming empty padding. Repeating the noun is not itself a defect.

Confidence is high. Frequency estimates from the whole archive would be misleading because duplicate documents and copied assignment questions also repeat language.

### 9. His short operational writing is noticeably different

The philanthropy note is a sequence of times, locations, point values, and actions. It includes:

> I will handle details tomorrow afternoon

File 35, "Bracket Challenge" section.

This supports direct ownership and compact practical formatting when the task calls for it. It does not justify writing explanatory essays as bullet lists. Nor does one short note establish a complete present-day email style.

Confidence is moderate for the mode and low for generalizing its capitalization or formatting to other genres.

## A small quantitative check

The following measurements use only the body prose of files 1, 3, 11, 13, 20, 26, 27, 46, 56, 57, and 64. Titles, headers, references, and trailing links were removed. These documents span reviews, personal reflection, ethical argument, and supply-chain explanation. Repeated copies were excluded.

| Measure | Result |
|---|---:|
| Approximate word count | 6,090 |
| Sentence count | 295 |
| Mean sentence length | 20.6 words |
| Median sentence length | 20 words |
| Sentences shorter than 10 words | 22 |
| Sentences longer than 30 words | 35 |
| Occurrences of "however" | 33 |
| Occurrences of "I believe" | 12 |
| Occurrences of "I think" | 5 |

Sentences were split after terminal punctuation and whitespace; words were counted with a simple tokenizer. These are approximate descriptive measurements, not stylometric identification or an authorship test. The sample was deliberately selected for interpretable individual prose and is not a random sample of everything Sam writes.

The check supports what the reading suggests: mostly medium-length sentences, some longer explanations, and a smaller number of short statements. A style built around constant sentence fragments would depart from this evidence.

## What was excluded or given less weight

### Primary individual prose

The curated reviews and completed marketing responses were the starting point. The longer individual English papers and reflections, ethics essays, Human Factors lab report, International Business reflection, and supply-chain writing supplied the strongest comparisons. Technical answers contributed short examples of definition, mechanism, and limitation.

Curated file 6, Marketing A2, contains a heading and unanswered questions. It supplies no substantive Sam-authored answer. The prompts in the other marketing files were excluded from style inference.

### Duplicate and related versions

Identical normalized extracted text appears in files 1/37, 2/12/33, 3/38, 11/55, and 70/76. Each copy was read, but each group supplies one independent observation. The executive-summary PDF and Word file repeat the same body prose. Several resume and cover-letter PDFs similarly repeat Word versions while adding extraction differences. The Mini Lit Review and Literature Review overlap extensively and represent a revision family, not two independent confirmations of every shared sentence.

### Group and copied material

Files 40, 41, 42, and 67 indicate team or multiple-author work. Without an identified contribution, their prose was excluded from the core model. Study guides in files 43, 50, 59, and 60 include definitions and lecture-like material whose wording cannot be safely attributed to Sam. Quoted passages, assignment instructions, equations, code, numeric tables, and software-generated output were not treated as ordinary prose evidence.

File 36 is a collection of fraternity questions with uncertain original composition. It is insufficient evidence for Sam's habitual humor or use of profanity.

### Uncertain individual or commercial material

File 63, the climate-change essay, differs from the stronger individual samples in its impersonal generalities and broad concluding rhetoric. It was held out of the core model pending authorship clarification. This is an evidence-weighting decision, not proof that it was AI-generated.

Files 68–83, the career Word and PDF documents, were read as secondary professional-writing evidence because editing or AI assistance is unresolved. File 84, the Casa Acciaio insert, is also secondary and uncertain; its brand-story language is not used as the default voice.

File 64's personal account is stronger voice evidence than its general introductory description of the bullwhip effect. That distinction matters even within a useful document.

### Archive narration and generated structures

Files 85–114 contain templates, indexes, structured notes, third-person narration about Sam, editing guidance, drafted STAR interview stories, or tailored resume derivatives. Their narration was excluded from the voice model. Phrases such as "per Sam," "Sam's characterization," and directions to future writers provide internal evidence of an intermediary's role.

A few archive passages are labeled as verbatim remarks or LinkedIn bullets. They offer limited corroboration, but their transcription and editing history are not independently established. The profile does not depend on them.

## Demonstrations using hypothetical facts

These are newly written examples, not quotations from Sam or claims about work he has done. They show how the guide changes drafting. Their resemblance has not yet been evaluated by Sam.

### Explaining a recommendation

Hypothetical brief: a weekly report copies the same numbers into three spreadsheets; propose one shared source, but confirm whether teams define the numbers the same way.

> I think the first thing we should look at is where these numbers are coming from. Right now, the same information is being copied into three different spreadsheets, so even a small change creates work for everyone using it. Keeping that information in one place would make the process much simpler. The part I would want to confirm first is whether each team means the same thing by those numbers. If they don't, putting everything together could make the report easier to maintain while making it harder to understand.

The example preserves a personal judgment, a cause, a concrete consequence, and a limiting condition. Its sentences develop the thought rather than compress it into slogans.

### Reflecting on a result

Hypothetical brief: a team expected slow execution to be the problem, but discovered it repeatedly started work without agreeing on the result.

> Going into this, I thought the biggest problem was how long the work was taking. Once we walked through a few examples, it became pretty clear that we were starting before everyone agreed on what we were trying to accomplish. That explained a lot. People were getting their part done, but their assumptions were different enough that we kept having to go back and change things. I think spending a little more time up front would have saved us a lot of that work.

The initial assumption changes through a specific observation. The short sentence marks the realization without taking over the paragraph's rhythm.

### Critiquing a result

Hypothetical brief: a scheduling experiment reduced average waiting time; question whether the benefit persists on unusually busy days.

> I think the reduction in waiting time is a good result, especially since the scheduling process itself stayed fairly simple. I would be interested to see how it performed on the busiest days, however. An average can make the process look consistent even if a small number of people are still waiting much longer than everyone else. Looking at those days separately would help explain whether the new schedule can handle the variation in demand.

The follow-up question changes one relevant condition. It gives the reader a reason to care about further testing.

## Limits and refinement

This corpus is rich in college-era individual writing and sparse in confirmed contemporary everyday writing. It supports a detailed historical profile, with cautious adaptations for current professional use. It does not establish a universal preference for openings, sign-offs, emojis, social-media hooks, or all possible audiences.

The next useful validation is Sam editing a real draft and identifying the sentences that sound unlike him. Accepted revisions should become new evidence, dated and associated with their genre. That is how this guide can become more accurate without drifting toward an assistant's preferred idea of good writing.
