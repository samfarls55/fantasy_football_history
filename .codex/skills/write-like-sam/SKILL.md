# Write in Sam's voice

Use this guide when Sam asks you to write or revise text in his voice. Apply it to the requested artifact, not automatically to your own conversation with him.

Sam's voice is practical and personable. He gives the reader enough context to understand the situation, makes his question or judgment clear, and explains how things work when that explanation is useful. A routine reply may be one sentence. A thoughtful explanation may need several connected paragraphs.

## Drafting workflow

### 1. Establish the brief

Identify the format, recipient, relationship, purpose, and facts available in the request or conversation. Identify the response Sam wants from the reader, or the understanding he wants them to leave with.

Use the samples as evidence of expression only. Do not import old experiences, opinions, career claims, contact details, or commitments into a new draft. Mark invented illustrations as hypothetical.

Follow Sam's current directions and explicit corrections over inferred style habits. Ask a question only when a missing fact materially changes the content; otherwise draft with the available facts.

Proceed when you can state the piece's purpose and distinguish supplied facts from unknowns.

### 2. Select the writing mode

Choose the closest row, then read its section below. A mixed task can combine modes, such as a short email containing technical feedback.

| Requested writing | Use |
|---|---|
| Email, reply, scheduling, confirmation, follow-up, refusal | Short correspondence |
| Confusion, correction, unresolved issue, dispute | Short correspondence, plus clarification and firmness |
| Familiar note or feedback to someone | Personal notes and advice |
| Explanation, recommendation, technical account, research critique | Explanations and recommendations |
| Personal essay or account of learning | Reflection |
| Resume, promotional copy, social post | Other formats |

Choose length from the purpose and what the reader already knows. An existing thread usually needs less context than a first message. Expand when a cause, discrepancy, or recommendation needs explanation. Treat word counts from the evidence as observations, not targets.

Proceed when you have selected the mode and identified what context the reader actually needs.

### 3. Draft using that mode

Apply the selected section and the shared language rules. Use the sample phrases only where they express the intended thought. They are examples of cadence, not a checklist of words to insert.

Finish this step when the draft answers the request and the reader can identify its point, question, or next action.

### 4. Review and return

Apply the completion checks below. Revise any failed check, then return the requested writing. Explain the stylistic choices only if Sam asks.

## Short correspondence

Start close to the purpose. Use whichever parts are needed: greeting or acknowledgment, relevant situation, specific question or answer, and a natural closing. A simple confirmation can stop after the answer.

- For a known recipient, "Hi [first name]," is a useful default. Use "Hey" with a familiar relationship. Match established forms of address and use time-of-day greetings only when appropriate.
- Keep paragraphs short, often one or two sentences. Natural fragments fit ongoing exchanges: "Just following up on this. Thanks!" or "Saw the invite come through".
- Allow conversational lead-ins such as "Just wanted to", "A quick question", "I was wondering", "Curious if", and "Can you please". Choose one when it fits; move directly into the point.
- Make requests answerable. Ask which paperwork is needed, whether a date works, what the next step is, or why an expected result differs from what happened.
- Show warmth through relevant appreciation: "Thanks for the quick reply", "Really appreciated our conversation", or "Looking forward to connecting!" A thank-you note can mention a specific idea from the conversation if it was supplied.
- Close with "Thanks!", "Thank you!", "Thanks again!", or a relevant next step. An ongoing reply may need no closing. Add a contact signature only when requested or supplied for use.

For scheduling, state the available time or confirm the proposed one. Offer alternatives when the facts support them. For a follow-up, restate only the missing answer or action.

For a refusal, state the decision clearly and keep courtesy proportionate to the relationship. If Sam is not interested, do not imply future interest, offer a call, or invent an excuse. Warmth must preserve his intent.

### Clarification and firmness

When something is unclear, state what Sam observed, what he expected or understood, and what needs explaining. "I noticed...", "My understanding is...", and "Can you help me understand...?" fit this structure.

When an earlier request was missed, name the missing item directly. "As requested in the original" can fit a repeated request. Keep the explanation specific enough for the recipient to resolve it.

Acknowledge a mistake plainly when the facts establish it. "Likely my mistake" can lead into a proposed fix. Apologize for the actual problem, without inventing fault.

Match firmness to Sam's direction and the history of the issue. His emails support both polite clarification and blunt disagreement. A firm response should state the boundary and the reason; use frustration only when the brief warrants it.

## Personal notes and advice

With familiar people, use ordinary conversational language and allow very short questions. Authentic examples include "You handling this?", "good to go", "really bummed out", and "Thanks man". Choose wording that fits the specific relationship.

For advice, give the concrete fix and explain why it matters. Use a numbered list for separate corrections and a paragraph when the recipient needs context or encouragement. Be candid about the work while considering the person's situation.

Use humor sparingly, when it follows naturally from the material. Sam's humor can be self-directed or point out an obvious incentive. Emoji evidence is limited; add them when the request or relationship supports them.

## Explanations and recommendations

Make the reasoning visible. Name what changes, explain how it affects the next part, and connect that mechanism to the conclusion. For a busy reader making a decision, state the recommendation first and follow with the reason.

Use complete, moderately long sentences, mixed with occasional short judgments. Keep each paragraph on one idea long enough to explain it. A longer sentence can connect a claim to a cause or limitation; split it when the reader would need to backtrack.

Use "I think" or "I believe" to own a judgment. State facts directly. Commit to a recommendation while naming any specific condition that could change it.

Use a concrete example when an abstract idea needs one. Explain the relationship the example illustrates, then return to the point. An example is optional when the mechanism is already clear.

For technical writing, define unfamiliar terms, describe the relevant steps, and state assumptions that affect the result. For research critique, separate the source's findings from Sam's interpretation and ask whether the conclusion holds under a specific changed condition.

Finish with the conclusion, decision, or practical implication.

## Reflection

Begin with the supplied experience or initial reaction. Where the facts support it, move through expectation, what happened, revised understanding, and what Sam would do differently.

Keep admissions ordinary and specific. Let the actual experience carry the emotion. A change of mind is useful when one occurred; do not invent a realization to complete the structure.

Use the connected sentence rhythm of longer explanations and allow occasional candid humor. End with what the experience means or changes.

## Other formats

For resumes and promotional writing, use concrete actions and outcomes with clear ownership. Follow the format's conventions while retaining ordinary language and visible reasoning where space permits.

For social or creative writing, adapt the closest supported mode to the request. Evidence is weaker for these formats, so avoid inventing a distinctive public persona or treating polished career materials as the default voice.

## Shared language rules

- Use familiar verbs, concrete nouns, and natural contractions. Keep technical terms when they name the actual concept.
- Keep key nouns consistent. Repeat "process" or "study" when that makes the thought easier to follow. Retain restatement when it explains a consequence; cut it when it repeats the same point.
- Connect ideas with words such as "because", "so", "however", and "although" when the relationship calls for them.
- Preserve useful qualifications. Name the condition or uncertainty rather than weakening every sentence.
- Allow "really", "definitely", and similar emphasis when they match the intended feeling. Preserve "Please let me know" and "Hope this helps!" when they serve the message. Generic anti-AI phrase bans should not erase observed habits.
- Use periods and commas for the basic rhythm. Exclamation marks fit thanks, friendly confirmations, and anticipation. Doubled exclamation marks can convey extra warmth occasionally. Use other punctuation and emphasis sparingly.
- Correct typos and grammar while preserving cadence. Match voice through reasoning, phrasing, and proportion rather than deliberate mistakes.
- Use headings and lists when they help the reader navigate or act. Let short messages remain short and let longer thoughts develop in paragraphs.

## Completion checks

Before returning the draft, check every applicable item:

- **Intent:** The draft expresses Sam's requested position. Courtesy has not added interest, willingness, apologies, or promises he did not authorize.
- **Facts:** Every personal claim, experience, number, and commitment comes from the brief or relevant conversation. Hypothetical examples are recognizable as hypothetical.
- **Proportion:** Each sentence supplies an answer, needed context, reasoning, or appropriate warmth. Remove introductions and summaries that repeat what the reader already knows.
- **Mode:** A short reply gets to its point promptly. A longer explanation shows how the conclusion follows. Neither uses the other's structure by habit.
- **Tone:** Greeting, familiarity, enthusiasm, and firmness fit the recipient and situation. Stock phrases appear because they fit, not to demonstrate the profile.
- **Reasoning:** Any caveat names a real uncertainty. Any example clarifies the point. Terms stay consistent.
- **Ending:** The piece stops once the question, decision, or thought is complete.
